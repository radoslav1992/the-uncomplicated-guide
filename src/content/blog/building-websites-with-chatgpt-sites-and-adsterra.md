*Build something useful, put ads on it, and hope Google eventually remembers you exist.*

I have built quite a few websites.

Some are complicated SaaS applications with databases, authentication, subscriptions, APIs, background jobs, and enough infrastructure to make you question your career choices.

Others are essentially HTML, CSS, and JavaScript.

And here's the annoying part.

**Sometimes the second category makes more economic sense.**

No database. No authentication. No expensive AI API calls. No monthly infrastructure bill that grows every time someone decides to use your product.

Just a website that does something useful.

And advertisements.

Welcome to another side hustle experiment.

## The Idea: Build Small, Useful Websites

The concept is almost offensively simple.

Build a website that solves a specific problem, attract visitors through search engines, and monetize the traffic with advertisements.

For example:

- A physics simulator that helps students understand motion.
- An interactive geometry calculator.
- A unit converter with visual explanations.
- A small browser game.
- A financial calculator that doesn't require creating an account just to divide two numbers.

I've already experimented with educational websites, including projects around physics, biology, astronomy, and geometry.

The common characteristic?

**Most of the functionality runs entirely in the user's browser.**

Which means my server costs aren't directly proportional to the number of people clicking buttons.

A refreshing concept after building AI applications where every interaction potentially sends another invoice to my inbox.

## Step 1: Use ChatGPT Sites to Build It

Traditionally, you would open your IDE, initialize a project, install dependencies, configure hosting, and spend half the evening arguing with a build tool.

Now, there is [ChatGPT Sites](https://chatgpt.com/features/sites/).

You describe the website you want, iterate on the design and functionality, preview it, and publish it.

I would start with something like:

> Build an interactive physics website that lets students experiment with gravity, velocity, and projectile motion. All calculations must run client-side. No accounts, databases, or paid APIs. Make it responsive, educational, and optimized for search engines.

Then refine it.

Improve the design, add explanations, test the calculations, and make sure the mobile experience doesn't resemble a spreadsheet that fell down the stairs.

One important distinction: ChatGPT Sites can publish lightweight websites, but I would verify support for my chosen third-party advertising scripts before committing to that hosting setup. If necessary, I'd use ChatGPT to build the project and deploy a compatible static version to Cloudflare or another host where I control the HTML and scripts.

The objective is to keep things simple, not develop a religious attachment to a deployment platform.

## Step 3: The Economics

Here comes the part where most side-hustle influencers would show you a screenshot of a Lamborghini.

Unfortunately, we're going to use mathematics.

Advertising revenue depends on traffic volume, geography, ad formats, advertiser demand, and several other factors.

Let's assume a hypothetical **$2 revenue per 1,000 pageviews**.

Not a promise, not an Adsterra quote. Just a number for an example.

| Monthly pageviews | Monthly revenue |
| ----------------- | --------------- |
| 10,000            | $20             |
| 50,000            | $100            |
| 100,000           | $200            |
| 500,000           | $1,000          |
| 1,000,000         | $2,000          |

Suddenly, our revolutionary business earns $20 a month.

Fantastic.

But consider the costs.

A static website can often be hosted on a free tier. You might spend around $10–20 annually on a domain, depending on the extension, with minimal infrastructure expenses beyond that.

If the website earns $20 a month and costs very little to operate, the cash margin can be attractive.

Of course, this ignores the 40 hours you spent building it.

We don't talk about those.

That's entrepreneurial accounting.

More seriously, your time, taxes, AI subscriptions, and any advertising or content costs still matter when calculating actual profit.

## Step 4: Build a Portfolio, Not a Masterpiece

This is where I find the model particularly interesting.

Instead of investing six months into one complicated project, build several genuinely useful websites.

For example:

**Website 1:** A collection of interactive math tools.

**Website 2:** A physics simulation playground.

**Website 3:** A simple finance calculator.

**Website 4:** A browser-based puzzle game.

Launch them, monitor traffic, improve the promising ones, and stop investing in the ones nobody uses.

In theory, ten small websites earning $50 per month each would produce $500 monthly.

In practice, eight might earn nothing, one might earn $3, and one might unexpectedly attract enough traffic to make the entire experiment worthwhile.

The internet is extremely generous with disappointment.

## Step 5: The Real Problem Is Traffic

Here's something I've learned after building more websites than any reasonable person needs.

**Building the website is usually the easy part.**

Getting people to visit is considerably harder.

You still need search engine optimization, useful content, discoverable pages, internal linking, and a reason for someone to choose your tool instead of the hundreds of alternatives.

And no, creating 500 nearly identical AI-generated pages is not a brilliant SEO strategy.

It's a brilliant way to create 500 pages nobody wants.

I would focus on genuinely useful interactive experiences that are difficult to replace with a paragraph of text.

Also, remember that adding an advertising network means considering privacy disclosures, consent requirements, and the experience of your visitors.

Cheap hosting does not exempt you from laws or common sense.

Unfortunately.

## Why This Side Hustle Makes Sense to Me

I like building software.

I also have a habit of finishing something and immediately wanting to build something else.

With traditional SaaS, that's a terrible business strategy.

Every abandoned application leaves behind infrastructure, subscriptions, maintenance, and occasionally angry error logs.

With small client-side websites, the downside can be much smaller.

Build something useful, publish it, monetize it, and let it exist without requiring constant attention.

This is not guaranteed passive income.

You still need traffic, updates, and occasional maintenance. And even a growing website might never earn meaningful money from ads.

But I like the economics of experiments that cost almost nothing to keep alive.

**The goal isn't to build the next billion-dollar startup.**

It's to build useful little internet properties that might collectively generate a few hundred dollars each month.

And if one unexpectedly becomes successful?

Naturally, I will celebrate by opening ChatGPT and starting another website.

Because apparently, learning when to stop building is a completely separate side hustle.
