The most common mistake in AI video is spending the afternoon on the avatar. The avatar is the part that feels like magic, so it gets the attention. Meanwhile the video opens with *In today's fast-paced world*, and nobody sees the avatar at all, because they are already looking at the next post.

A short-form feed makes its decision early. Whatever the ranking systems do downstream, they cannot rank a video that people swipe past in the first second, and the share of viewers still watching at three seconds is the number that moves everything after it. Which means your production pipeline has one job before all the others: give the first three seconds something worth staying for.

## Four ways AI video announces itself

Viewers cannot articulate why a video feels off, but they leave anyway. In practice it is nearly always one of these:

**The script is about the product.** A model asked for *an ad for a dental clinic* returns something that reads like the About page set to music. Real ads open in the middle of something: a problem, a number, a contradiction, an admission.

**The delivery is flat.** Synthetic speech performs punctuation, not meaning. A wall of text with no commas, no sentence breaks and no pacing gets read at an even canter that sounds like a train announcement. Fixing the writing fixes most of the voice.

**The lips drift on the third sentence.** Lip sync tends to hold for a few seconds and then loosen, particularly around numbers, names and anything the audio rushes. It is worth watching the render at full size once, specifically for the mouth, before anything else.

**The captions sit under the interface.** Every platform puts its own furniture over the bottom of the frame, and the exact height changes with each app update. Captions placed by an editor that does not know this will be half-covered on the phone even though they looked fine on the laptop.

None of those four is fixed by a better avatar.

## Test hooks, not videos

The useful unit of work is not a video. It is one body with five openings.

Write the body once, get it right, then produce five hooks against it: a question, a number, a mistake, a before-and-after, and a flat contradiction of what the audience assumes. Same voice, same avatar, same edit. The only variable is the first three seconds.

Publish all five. Within a couple of days it is usually obvious which one holds attention, and it is frequently not the one you liked in the edit. The point of the pipeline is that producing the other four costs you almost nothing once the body exists — which is the actual business case for making these with AI, far more than the saving on a film crew.

## Measure the thing that decides

Likes tell you nothing you can act on. Three numbers do:

- **Hook rate** — the share still watching at three seconds. Diagnoses the opening.
- **Hold** — where the graph falls off a cliff. That timestamp is a line in your script, and you can go and read it.
- **Cost per result** — whatever the client actually sells, per pound spent. Everything else is decoration.

Given those three, a failing video tells you which link of the chain broke. Without them, you are re-editing on taste.

## Label it

Platforms want AI-generated content marked, and advertising rules increasingly want it too. The label is not what loses the viewer — the second sentence is. And the alternative, a testimonial from a customer who does not exist, is not a risk worth taking: in most markets that is not merely against a platform policy, it is against the law.

Disclose it, write something worth watching, and put the effort where the decision is actually made: in the first three seconds.
