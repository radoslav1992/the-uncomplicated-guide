Most services people sell have costs that sit still. You do the work once, you send the invoice, the cost of delivering it next month is roughly the cost of delivering it this month.

AI services are not like that. A voice agent costs you something every minute it is on a call. A video pipeline costs you something every time it renders. Which means the bill you receive is set by how well your client's business is going, and the bill you send, if you priced it the usual way, is not.

That gap is where people lose money without noticing, because it opens slowly and it opens fastest on the clients they are most pleased to have.

## Separate what happens once from what happens monthly

Two different things get bundled into one number and should not be.

**The build** happens once: the discovery conversation, the prompt, the connection to a calendar or a catalogue, the number, the testing, the handover. It costs you time, it does not recur, and it is worth what it is worth to the client whether they keep the service for two months or two years.

**The running** happens every month: the usage that scales, the platform subscriptions, the checks, the small fixes, the report. This is the part exposed to volume.

Charge for them separately. Bundling the build into a low monthly fee to make the first invoice look friendly means you have financed the client's setup and can only recover it if they stay — which they are least likely to do if the thing is not working, which is exactly when you have spent the most.

## Do the arithmetic on a bad month

Not an average month. The worst plausible one.

Write the running cost as what it is: a fixed part plus a variable part.

```
monthly cost = subscriptions + (usage × cost per unit)
```

Say your all-in variable cost is around 15 cents a minute — telephony, transcription, model, speech, rounded up rather than down — and your fixed platform spend attributable to that client is $40. The client expects about 800 minutes a month. A quiet month of 300 minutes costs you $85, which against a $400 fee is a 79% margin, and you feel clever.

Then the client puts the number on a van, or a competitor down the road closes, and they run 3,000 minutes — roughly four times what either of you expected. Now it costs you $490, and you are paying $90 a month for the privilege of being their supplier. Nothing went wrong. It went right.

Run that second calculation *before* quoting, at three or four times the volume the client expects. If the answer is uncomfortable, the price structure is wrong — not the price.

## Cap it, and say so at the start

The fix is not a bigger flat fee. It is a fee that includes a stated allowance, with what happens beyond it written down before anyone signs.

> $400 a month includes up to 800 minutes. Above that, 25 cents a minute, billed monthly.

Two sentences. They do the work of a difficult email six months from now, and they cost nothing to say up front, when the client is comparing you with someone who has not thought about it and will later have to have that awkward conversation instead.

Set the included allowance somewhere near what the client actually expects to use, not at the ceiling. An allowance nobody reaches is a discount you gave away for nothing; an allowance everybody blows through by the fourth of the month is an argument you have every month.

## Price the outcome, meter the usage

Both halves matter. Meter the usage so the cost side never runs away from you. But do not price the service *as* the usage, because minutes are not what the client is buying, and a price expressed purely per minute invites them to compare you with the cheapest possible source of minutes.

What they are buying is that the phone gets answered, or that ten tested creatives arrive every month. That is the thing with a value attached, and it is worth defending: if answering the calls that currently go unanswered is worth several thousand a month to them, the conversation about your fee is not really a conversation about your costs.

## When to walk away

Sometimes the arithmetic simply does not work. Very long calls with a low value per call, a client whose volume is unpredictable and whose budget is fixed, an offer where each conversion is worth less than what it costs you to have the conversation.

Recognising this early is worth as much as any pricing model. The unprofitable client rarely arrives labelled — they arrive enthusiastic, ask for a start next week, and only become a problem in month four. Doing the bad-month calculation during the first conversation is what tells them apart from the good ones, and it takes about five minutes.
